# The packet in plain English

Working notes on the Field Signal packet (Project HC-17), written for someone
with no construction background. Terms are defined in italics the first time
they appear.

## The setting

A café is being built inside an existing building in Portland. It's a
**fit-out** (*building the inside of an empty shell — walls, wiring, kitchen,
furniture — the building itself already exists*). Work started 3 August. The
owner wants staff training on 5 October and doors open to the public on
12 October. Everything in the packet is one single working day:
**Monday 14 September**.

The trouble is all happening in one specific spot: the **north soffit**.

> **Soffit** = a box built out of the ceiling, hanging lower than the rest,
> that hides pipes and ducts inside it. Think of the boxed-in bit above a
> kitchen counter or the lowered strip above a bar. It looks like
> architecture; it's really a hiding place for services.

This particular soffit is above the service counter, and it's crowded. Stuffed
inside it: air-conditioning ducts, sprinkler pipes, lighting, and hatches to
get back in later.

## The physical problem, in one line

**Two things need the same space, and only one can have it.**

An air duct branch (the pipe carrying cold air to a ceiling vent) and a fire
sprinkler head (the little brass thing that sprays water if there's a fire)
both want the same pocket inside that soffit. They physically cannot both go
there.

> **Rough-in** = pipes and ducts installed *before* walls and ceilings get
> closed up. Cheap and easy now, expensive later.
> **Close-in** = putting the ceiling on and sealing it all up. After this,
> fixing anything means cutting a hole.
> **Above-ceiling inspection** = a city inspector climbs a ladder and checks
> the hidden work before you're allowed to cover it.
> **Diffuser** = the vent grille you see in a ceiling.

This is why it's urgent. There's a fixed sequence with no slack in it:

**Thursday 17th** — inspector checks the hidden work → **Friday 18th** —
ceiling gets boarded up → then paint, then floors, then furniture, then
opening day.

If the clash isn't sorted out, the inspector can't sign off. If he can't sign
off, the ceiling can't close. If the ceiling can't close, everything behind it
slides — and at the end of that queue is a 12 October opening date the owner
has presumably already told people about.

## The three things fighting over that soffit

1. **The duct branch + diffuser** — Ben's. This is what he moved ~6″ west on
   Sunday. "Reversible" describes *this*: it's hung loose, no final tap into
   the trunk, no diffuser fitted, no ceiling cut. He can put it back in an
   hour if the architect says no.
2. **The sprinkler head** — Omar's. Not installed, not even laid out.
3. **The access hatch** — nobody's yet.

> **Access panel / hatch** = a removable square in the finished ceiling so
> someone can reach the valves and dampers hidden inside the soffit later,
> without smashing the ceiling open.

The hatch is a *design* item, not something Ben installed. Priya's note points
at the **reflected ceiling plan** (*the drawing showing the ceiling as if
looking down at a mirror on the floor — where lights, vents and hatches sit*),
which shows it centred between the lights.

And here's why it matters — it's the actual jam:

- Priya won't confirm the duct move until she knows where the hatch goes
  ("please include proposed access-panel location")
- Omar won't finalise his sprinkler head until he knows where the hatch ends
  up ("I need to see where the access panel ends up")
- Ben can't make his final connection until both of those land

So a piece of ceiling trim that nobody has drawn yet is holding up two foremen
and a $2,850 decision. Ben's "reversible" is the only thing keeping this
cheap — the moment that duct gets tapped and connected, or the ceiling boards
go on, the hatch position stops being a free choice.

Worth noting: the packet never resolves where the hatch goes. That's an
**unknown**, not an inference.

## The seven people and what each of them is doing

**Ben Walker — the air-conditioning foreman (Cascade Air).**
On **Sunday**, on his own initiative, he moved the duct roughly 6 inches west
so the wall-framers weren't blocked. He left it loose and reversible, didn't
make the final connection. He'd already sent a price to do the job properly:
**$2,850**. He's now sitting there saying *I need an answer today or I lose my
crew.*

**Maya Chen — the project manager (the main contractor, Northline).**
She's the only person who can approve extra money. The written rule:
**anything over $2,000 needs Maya's written authorisation.** She's holding the
line — no final connection, get the architect and the fire guy to look at the
actual condition first. She has not approved the $2,850.

**Luis Ortega — the superintendent (also Northline).**
The one running the site day-to-day. He can rearrange *who works where and
when*; he explicitly **cannot** approve extra cost. He's the one under
schedule pressure, and at one point he asks Maya to "call it proceed pending
paperwork" — i.e. *let's start and do the forms later.* She says no.

**Omar Ellis — the fire sprinkler foreman (Sentinel Fire).**
Quietly the most important person nobody has resolved. He says he told them he
needed room but **never finalised where his sprinkler head goes** — and he
can't decide until he knows where the access hatch ends up. He looked at
Luis's photo and said: *if the duct is where that photo shows, it's tight.
Maybe okay, maybe not.* Ben immediately points out the photo is out of date.

**Priya Shah — the architect (Atelier North).**
Asked on Friday to confirm whether the duct is allowed to shift. She replied
"I'll review Monday morning" and asked where the access hatch is going. She
can approve the *design*, not the *money*.

**Tasha Reed — the owner's representative.**
Cares about one thing: opening on time. Says "if it's under three grand I'd
rather keep moving." But under the project's own rules **she is not allowed to
authorise a subcontractor's change** — so her blessing, however encouraging,
is worth nothing contractually. She also later calls it "a two-thousand-dollar
issue" when the quote is actually $2,850.

**Nina Alvarez — the furniture/joinery PM (Timberline).**
A side plot. Luis heard two different delivery dates. Nina clarifies: the
shipment was split — counters Tuesday, curved booth Thursday — and insists
this isn't new, it's per "the approved recovery schedule." That recovery
schedule **is not in the packet**, so her explanation can't be checked.

## The actual problem worth building around

Strip out the construction and it's this:

> **Work has been done. Money is owed. Nobody with the authority to approve
> either has approved anything. And the one meeting that was supposed to
> resolve it has no record of taking place.**

Every person in that story is behaving reasonably from their own seat. Ben
unblocked the framers. Luis protected the schedule. Tasha protected the
opening. Maya protected the contract. Omar refused to commit to something he
couldn't see. Priya asked a fair question. Nobody did anything stupid — and
yet at the end of the day the project has:

- a duct in a new position nobody signed off on
- a $2,850 bill nobody authorised
- an architect's review that may or may not have happened
- a fire sprinkler layout that is still genuinely undecided
- an inspection in 3 days that depends on all of the above
- and the owner cheerfully saying "sounds fine to me" about something she
  isn't permitted to approve

That's the thing that gets lost. Not because anyone hid it — because it lived
in a huddle nobody wrote down, a text thread, and a photo that went stale in
48 hours.

## The traps, in plain terms

**"Six inches west" is three different numbers.** Ben's text says about 6. Ben
in the meeting says six or eight. His own quote says 6–12. Nobody measured it.
If your product prints a number, it's inventing one.

**A photo doesn't prove anything.** Ben's 10:18 photo shows a duct. It does
not show that moving it was allowed, that it's finished, how far it moved, or
that it meets fire code. And the caption under it is Ben describing his own
work.

**A schedule is a plan, not a receipt.** The spreadsheet says the inspection
is Thursday. That's an intention. It isn't evidence that anything on it
actually happened — the file literally says so at the bottom.

**Nothing happening is not the same as nothing existing.** There's no record
of Priya's review. That means *we don't know*, not *she didn't do it*. Getting
this distinction right is a big chunk of the marks.

**Twelve seconds of the recording are just noise.** At 08:11 the transcript
catches "…access… twenty-four… north" over a compressor. It might be a
24-inch clearance requirement, which would matter enormously. It might be
nothing. It is not recoverable, and pretending otherwise is exactly the
failure they're testing for.

**$2,850 is not the real cost.** The quote explicitly excludes patching the
drywall, repainting, any sprinkler rework, and any redesign. So the true
number is higher and currently unknowable.
